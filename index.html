Pathfinder Design System

Avant's first unified design system — token-driven, dual-themed, built from zero, and owned end to end for three years.

01The Problem

Pathfinder — where technology advisors research, quote, and close enterprise deals — had grown faster than its design language. Three years of separate teams had layered five primary blues, three button shapes, and mixed type stacks into production, while marketing produced at high volume with no shared foundations.

In a tool where advisors make six-figure recommendations in front of clients, visual fragmentation undermines trust in the data itself. Inconsistency reads as unreliability.

The brief: one visual language, expressed as tokens and components, that product engineers and marketers could both ship from — without asking permission.

02What I Built

Audit first. A full interface inventory made fragmentation countable, not anecdotal — the system had a business case before a single component existed.

Three-tier tokens. Primitives → semantic → component. Engineers consume intent, never hex codes. Dark product surfaces and light marketing resolve from one semantic layer — re-theme from the primitive layer and nothing downstream changes.

Designed or it isn't done. Every component shipped with loading, empty, error, and disabled states — in a platform full of in-progress assessments, the in-between states are the product. A token-driven chart library (line, bar, area, stream, radar, hex) carried the platform's credibility layer: live revenue data.

Governance built for adoption. Federated contribution, docs written for the person shipping at 4pm Friday, weekly office hours that caught edge cases before they became forks, and semantic releases with migration notes.

03The Outcome

The library became the default path, not a mandate. Brand reviews shifted from policing basics to discussing ideas. New surfaces shipped visually consistent on day one, and accessibility findings stopped recurring because the fixes lived in the tokens. The architecture carried Pathfinder from 2.0 through 3.0 without a redesign.

When the same feedback keeps arriving, the fix is upstream in the system — not in the individual deliverables.
04Next — Pathfinder 3.0

The third act, in development. A concept pass evolving the brand and the system together — a deeper dark theme, richer sales and commerce surfaces, and denser data visualization, all resolving from the same token architecture that shipped 2.0.

The point of a system isn't to freeze a brand. It's to give evolution somewhere safe to happen.

01The Problem

After the product system unified Pathfinder, marketing was the next bottleneck: high asset volume, no shared foundations, every campaign re-litigating the basics. The design problem wasn't creating a new system — it was adapting one for non-designers, who need harder rails and warmer documentation.

02What I Built

One template, infinite expression. "Forge the Future" set the model: a locked typographic and brand structure with expressive visual fields, so campaign variants multiply without drift — dozens of banner and social formats from one template logic.

Beyond banners. The library covered the unglamorous, high-volume work: 40-page industry reports, partner enablement, and event material — templates carrying the brand's editorial layer so a report and a LinkedIn banner read as one company. The pattern throughout: I created the materials first — covers, reports, campaign assets — then refined each into the library as templates the team could ship from.

From the library — report cover explorations · final cover · Pinnacle loyalty microsite

Enablement over enforcement. Adoption ran through training sessions and self-serve guides written for non-designers. The training did more for scale than any template.

03The Outcome

The library became the default path because it was faster than the old way. Marketing stopped rebuilding basics, revision cycles on routine assets collapsed, and brand consistency stopped depending on any one person's vigilance — including mine.

Marketing libraries are design systems with a different user — the architecture is the same, the empathy is different.

01The Problem

BCBS member and enrollment experiences were built by many hands across many vendors — and it showed. Components were disjointed, specifications lived nowhere, and every new flow reconstructed the basics. For a healthcare brand whose entire promise is trust, the seams were the problem.

02What I Built

Foundations first. A color system with interaction and accessibility tiers, a typographic scale built for dense insurance content, an icon library, and grids that worked from marketing pages to enrollment forms.

Accessibility as architecture. In healthcare, accessibility isn't an enhancement — it's the product working or not working. Contrast, focus, and plain language were baked into the foundation layer, not added after.

Proven on the highest stakes. Plan selection and enrollment — forms, payment, and confirmation patterns shipped as coded components with theming hooks, with the same foundations carrying the marketing surfaces.

03The Outcome

Teams stopped reconstructing the basics. Enrollment flows shipped visually consistent. The guide gave designers and developers one vocabulary — and the system accelerated concept-to-completion across BCBS's multi-product ecosystem.

Healthcare taught me accessibility discipline — a standard I've carried into every system since.

01The Problem

PRISM measures how people think, work, and relate — psychometric data that's genuinely complex and personally sensitive. The design problem: visualize it so a manager can act on it in thirty seconds, without flattening the nuance or making anyone feel reduced to a chart.

02What I Built

Visualization as vocabulary. Relationship maps, trait spectrums, and team composites — each a reusable pattern with defined anatomy, not a one-off chart. The same components scale from an individual profile to a team view.

Desktop depth, mobile moments. Desktop carries the analytical depth; mobile carries onboarding, check-ins, and quick reads before a meeting. One modular kit, tokens shared, hierarchy re-tuned per context.

Warm by design. Psychometrics border on the personal — typography, color, and language did as much emotional work as the data itself.

03The Outcome

A cohesive UI framework across the full platform, with interaction models and information architecture defined for complex data visualization — and modular kits and tokens the team could keep building on after my engagement.

Data visualization is a component problem. Define the grammar, and the data speaks for itself.

01Audit First

TUCM started the way every honest system does: with an audit. I mapped every component, color, and pattern in production into a single research artifact that made the fragmentation undeniable — and gave the system its priorities. The audit isn't just discovery; it's the business case before a single component exists.

02What I Built

The system. Fully responsive symbol libraries — years before Figma variants, this was disciplined Sketch architecture — plus a written guide covering color, type, and library workflow, with foundations tuned for data-dense credit dashboards.

The consumer proof. TrueCredit: a rebrand and product design pass on TransUnion's consumer credit experience, built on TUCM foundations. Credit scores, calculators, and walkthroughs designed to make intimidating financial data feel navigable.

03The Outcome

Design-to-code workflows got a shared vocabulary. The marketing library unified brand touchpoints. Credit data — some of the most anxiety-inducing content on the internet — got an interface language built for clarity. The pattern of audit → argument → architecture has carried into every system since.

Financial UI is trust UI — hierarchy and plain numbers decide whether someone believes their own data.
