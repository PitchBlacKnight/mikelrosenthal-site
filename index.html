Avant Communications · B2B Technology · 2021 — 2024

Pathfinder Design System

Avant's first unified design system — dual-themed, built from zero, and owned end to end for three years.

01The Problem

Pathfinder — where technology advisors research, quote, and close enterprise deals — had grown faster than its design language. Three years of separate teams had layered five primary blues, three button shapes, and mixed type stacks into production, while marketing produced at high volume with no shared foundations.

In a tool where advisors make six-figure recommendations in front of clients, visual fragmentation undermines trust in the data itself. Inconsistency reads as unreliability.

The brief: one visual language, expressed as a component system that product engineers and marketers could both ship from — without asking permission.

02What I Built

Audit first. A full interface inventory made fragmentation countable, not anecdotal — the system had a business case before a single component existed.

Three-tier tokens. Primitives → semantic → component. Engineers consume intent, never hex codes. Dark product surfaces and light marketing resolve from one semantic layer — re-theme from the primitive layer and nothing downstream changes.

Designed or it isn't done. Every component shipped with loading, empty, error, and disabled states — in a platform full of in-progress assessments, the in-between states are the product. A themeable chart library (line, bar, area, stream, radar, hex) carried the platform's credibility layer: live revenue data.

Governance built for adoption. Federated contribution, docs written for the person shipping at 4pm Friday, weekly office hours that caught edge cases before they became forks, and semantic releases with migration notes.

03The Outcome

The library became the default path, not a mandate. Brand reviews shifted from policing basics to discussing ideas. New surfaces shipped visually consistent on day one, and accessibility findings stopped recurring because the fixes lived at the foundation level. The architecture carried Pathfinder from 2.0 through 3.0 without a redesign.

When the same feedback keeps arriving, the fix is upstream in the system — not in the individual deliverables.
04Next — Pathfinder 3.0

The third act, in development. A concept pass evolving the brand and the system together — a deeper dark theme, richer sales and commerce surfaces, and denser data visualization, all resolving from the same architecture that shipped 2.0.

Systems exist so a brand can keep evolving without breaking.

01The Problem

After the product system unified Pathfinder, marketing was the next bottleneck: high asset volume, no shared foundations, every campaign re-litigating the basics. The design problem wasn't creating a new system — it was adapting one for non-designers, who need harder rails and warmer documentation.

02What I Built

One template, infinite expression. "Forge the Future" set the model: a locked typographic and brand structure with expressive visual fields, so campaign variants multiply without drift — dozens of banner and social formats from one template logic.

From campaign to calendar. The same template logic carried event creative and the social calendar — the NSP Advisory Board '24 package ran from stage screens to badges to email from one Figma system, and holiday socials shipped on-brand without a designer bottleneck.

Beyond banners. The library covered the unglamorous, high-volume work: 40-page industry reports, partner enablement, and event material — templates carrying the brand's editorial layer so a report and a LinkedIn banner read as one company. The pattern throughout: I created the materials first — covers, reports, campaign assets — then refined each into the library as templates the team could ship from.

Enablement over enforcement. Adoption ran through training sessions and self-serve guides written for non-designers. The training did more for scale than any template.

03The Outcome

The library became the default path because it was faster than the old way. Marketing stopped rebuilding basics, revision cycles on routine assets collapsed, and brand consistency stopped depending on any one person's vigilance — including mine.

Marketing libraries are design systems with a different user — the architecture is the same, the empathy is different.

Pinnacle. The loyalty program microsite — tiered rewards told through altitude, from base level to summit, built entirely inside the library's template logic.

01The Problem

BCBS member and enrollment experiences were built by many hands across many vendors — and it showed. Components were disjointed, specifications lived nowhere, and every new flow reconstructed the basics. For a healthcare brand whose entire promise is trust, the seams were the problem.

02What I Built

Foundations first. A color system with interaction and accessibility tiers, a typographic scale built for dense insurance content, an icon library, and grids that worked from marketing pages to enrollment forms.

Accessibility as architecture. In healthcare, accessibility decides whether the product works at all. Contrast, focus, and plain language were baked into the foundation layer, not added after.

Proven on the highest stakes. Plan selection and enrollment — forms, payment, and confirmation patterns shipped as coded components with theming hooks, with the same foundations carrying the marketing surfaces.

Documented like a product. Corner radii, modals, shadows, buttons and states, form fields, spacing steps, the multi-plan logo matrix, and responsive minimums — one sheet a vendor could build from without a meeting.

The system in production. The under-65 shopping experience rebuilt as a conversation — one question per screen, plain language, progress you can leave and return to. Every screen below is assembled from FIBER foundations.

03The Outcome

Teams stopped reconstructing the basics. Enrollment flows shipped visually consistent. The guide gave designers and developers one vocabulary — and the system accelerated concept-to-completion across BCBS's multi-product ecosystem.

Healthcare taught me accessibility discipline — a standard I've carried into every system since.

01The Brief

Maven Wave needed a visual brand that could hold a hard middle ground: professional, high-quality, innovative, likable, and smart — a traditional consulting firm on one side, a cloud brokerage and technology firm on the other. The identity had to represent real capability and leave room to grow.

The presentation opened by teaching the client how to judge a logo — Paul Rand, the Citi case study, and the rule that a mark earns meaning by association: simple, memorable, timeless, versatile, appropriate.

02Four Directions

Each concept was built as a complete system, not a sketch — construction logic, mark, lockups on light and dark, a typeface cast to match the personality, and a physical mockup to make it real.

03The Outcome

Four directions, presented as one argument about what a brand is for. The lineup gave Maven Wave a real decision to make — mark, voice, and material presence together — and the firm carried its identity into becoming one of Google Cloud’s premier partners, later acquired by Atos.

A logo derives its meaning from the quality of what it symbolizes — the deck taught the principle, then proved it four ways.

RoleDesigner — brand ideation ScopeBrand exploration · Visual identity · Fintech

After 2017’s breach era, freezing your credit went from an obscure safeguard to a mainstream act. This ideation explored how a freeze product could look approachable instead of bureaucratic — friendly geometry, clear states, and a visual temperature that says protected, not punished.
