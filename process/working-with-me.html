Five phases, one decision gate each, and what comes out of every one

This is how I run an engagement: remote, in front of the client, on a Figma source of truth, handing off to engineers who build it exactly as shown. Four-minute read. Every phase fits on one screen, and the gate is the only line that matters.

Four commitments

What you can hold me to

01

No screen without a metric behind it

If we can’t say what number the feature is supposed to move, that’s a strategy conversation, not a design task. I’d rather spend an hour finding the metric than two weeks designing the wrong thing.

02

Everything is written down, in the file

Decisions, assumptions and open questions live next to the frames they affect, not in a call I was on and you weren’t. A remote team runs on written artifacts or it runs on re-litigation.

03

A recommendation, not a menu

Clients hold final say. That’s their right and their risk. My job is to make the choice easy: the option I’d ship, what it trades, and the one I rejected and why.

04

Handoff is a conversation, not a delivery

I stay in the sprint. Office hours for engineers, design review on staging, and I fix the spec when reality disagrees with it. It will.

The method

Each phase ends in a decision, not a deliverable

The phases compress or stretch with scope. An MVP runs this in six weeks. An enterprise platform takes twelve. The order never changes, and no phase closes without its gate. The gate protects the budget. It’s cheap to be wrong in phase 01 and ruinous in phase 05.

01 Frame

Week 1

Agree on the problem before anyone opens Figma. Most briefs arrive describing a solution. My first job is to find the problem underneath it and write down how we’ll know we solved it.

What I run

  • A 90-minute stakeholder alignment workshop
  • A constraints inventory with the lead engineer
  • A competitive teardown of three to five products

What you get

  • A one-page problem statement, in the client’s words
  • Success metrics mapped to business KPIs
  • A ranked assumption register, each with an owner
  • A research plan scoped to the decisions it informs

The gate

The client signs the problem statement and the metrics. If we can’t write the metric, we don’t design the feature yet. We go back to strategy.

02 Learn

Weeks 1–2

Replace the loudest opinion in the room with evidence, sized to the risk. Research is scoped to the decision it informs, never run as a checkbox.

What I run

  • Five to eight interviews, or a moderated pass on what exists
  • A heuristic evaluation of the current experience
  • Analytics review wherever there’s data
  • A card sort when IA is the actual question

What you get

  • Ten insights maximum, each with a design implication
  • A current-state journey map with friction marked
  • A friction list ranked by frequency times severity

The gate

Findings review with the client and PM. Every insight becomes a design constraint or gets parked on the record. Nothing sits in a deck doing nothing.

On timeline pressure: a two-day audit is right for a mature product with analytics. A week of interviews is right for a new flow with no users yet. I’ll tell you which one I’m buying and what it costs to skip the other.

03 Architect

Weeks 2–4

Get the structure right while it’s still cheap to change. Low fidelity on purpose. Grey boxes invite structural objections. Polished screens invite opinions about color.

What I run

  • Story map, then site map, then flows for the critical paths
  • Lo-fi wireframes of every state, not just the happy path
  • A content model pass: what data each screen needs

What you get

  • Annotated flow diagrams an engineer can estimate from
  • A complete lo-fi wireframe set
  • An edge-case matrix, the screens nobody asks for

The gate

Feasibility check with engineering, then a client walkthrough on lo-fi. Structural objections are free at this gate and expensive at the next one.

04 Design

Weeks 4–8

High-fidelity screens built on a system, not a pile of artboards. The library gets built as the screens get built. That’s the only way it survives contact with a deadline.

What I run

  • Two visual directions maximum, one recommended
  • Atomic order: tokens, then primitives, then components, then screens
  • Hi-fi screens for every state the wireframes named
  • A clickable prototype of the critical paths

What you get

  • A Figma library on variables and variants, named to match code
  • Visual guidelines: type, color roles, icons, imagery, motion
  • An interactive prototype, the thing that ends arguments

The gate

Internal design critique first, client review second. The team tears it apart before the client sees it. That’s the point of having a design team.

05 Validate

Weeks 8–12

Prove it works, then make it hard to build wrong. Usability testing before development is the cheapest insurance on the project. Friction found here costs hours, not sprints.

What I run

  • A five-participant moderated test, then a fix pass
  • A handoff walkthrough, where I present the system live
  • Office hours through the build, design QA on staging

What you get

  • Usability findings with severity ratings and a fix log
  • Annotated specs: spacing, behavior, breakpoints, accessibility
  • Token export, state matrix, and a design-QA checklist

The gate

Engineering confirms in writing that it can be built as specified. Then design QA signs off on staging before anything ships.

Handoff contract

Exactly what an engineer gets

When the promise to a client is that development builds it exactly as shown, the spec has to be good enough to make that true. This is the checklist I hand over, every time.

Tokens

Named to match code

Color roles, type scale, spacing, radii, elevation, as Figma Variables with the same names the front end uses. Exportable, not screenshotable.

Components

Every state, defined

Default, hover, focus-visible, active, disabled, loading, error, empty. If a state isn’t in the library it isn’t designed, and it will get invented in code.

Behavior

Rules, not redlines

Written as conditions, like “the rail collapses when the content column drops under 480px”, so layouts survive content the mockup never had.

Accessibility

Specified, not assumed

Focus order, roles and labels, contrast pairs recorded with their ratios, targets at 44px. WCAG 2.2 held as a gate, not a final-week audit.

Content

Real strings, real lengths

Microcopy written by me and reviewed with the client, including the error messages, the empty states, and the longest name in the database.

Presence

Me, during the build

A live walkthrough at handoff, weekly office hours, and design review on staging. The spec starts a conversation. It isn’t a wall to throw work over.

Working with others

Who I work with, and how

The part of the method that isn’t on a timeline. Six relationships, and what each one gets from me.

Engineers

I work in their tools

Dev Mode, Code Connect, Storybook, the repo. I validate the design source against what shipped, not against the library’s own claims. At Verizon I diffed tokens and variables against production code across two platform teams and caught the drift before release.

Product and business partners

A working screen beats a principle

I’d rather show a screen that works than argue a principle. When I recommend something, I say what it gives up and why. The client still decides. My job is to make the trade visible before they do.

Other designers

Adoption is the metric, not compliance

Reviews, office hours, and mentoring on correct usage and accessibility. FIBER’s 54 components were adopted by three product teams because the library answered their questions, not because a policy required it. Edison ran the same way across GE Healthcare’s matrixed org and 40-plus products.

Embedded and self-directed

The one systems person on a small team

At Avant I was the only design-systems contributor on a product team of three, with three engineers and a PM. Day to day that meant designing the application and servicing flows, proving each pattern in a real screen, and moving it into the library the same week. Nobody assigned the library work. It came out of the product work.

Handoff

Teams run the system without me

Standards, documentation and recorded reviews, so the system keeps working after I leave. FIBER had intake, a release cadence, versioning and deprecation written down. That’s what let three teams keep using it without a call to me.

Ambiguity protocol

When the brief contradicts itself

Client work means digesting information that is incomplete, changing, and sometimes contradicts itself. That isn’t a failure of the client. It’s the normal condition of the job. Four moves, in order.

01

Write the contradiction down, in their words

Two stakeholders wanting opposite things is a business decision wearing a design costume. I don’t quietly split the difference. I name both positions, show what each costs, and hand the choice back with a recommendation attached.

02

Separate the assumption from the constraint

Everything ambiguous goes on the assumption register with an owner and a cost of being wrong. Cheap-to-test assumptions get tested this week. Expensive ones get decided by the client, on the record, so nobody relitigates them in month three.

03

Build the smallest thing that settles it

A one-day clickable prototype ends arguments that a two-week email thread only deepens. When a debate has gone two rounds without new information, I stop debating and build the artifact that answers it.

04

Push back with a proposal attached

“I don’t think that works” is noise. “That will cost us the onboarding funnel. Here’s the alternative, here’s what it trades, here’s how we’d know in two weeks” is the job. Pushing back on a brief only earns trust when you arrive with the replacement.

Built from nothing, adopted by three product teams, documented like a product: intake, release cadence, versioning and deprecation. The coded library matched the design source in naming, structure and behavior. That parity is why the handoff contract above is as specific as it is. A component that doesn’t match its code is a suggestion.

What I got wrong

I used to take constraints into wireframes without testing them

I accepted a stated back-end constraint in week one, carried it into the structure, and only questioned it at hi-fi, where changing it is expensive. That is why a constraints inventory with the lead engineer is now part of phase 01 instead of something I get to the week after.

First 30 days

What I’d do in the first month

For a hiring manager picturing me in the seat. Nothing here needs a reorg.

01

Week one: read everything, then sit with an engineer

I read the brief, the library, the docs and the backlog with no design tool open. Then I sit with the lead engineer and inventory what actually ships against what the library claims. The gap between those two is the plan.

02

Week two: one visible fix

I pick the drift with the highest cost and fix the primitive once. A missing focus state, greys off the ramp, a component with three button variants in code. Small, shipped, and something the team can see.

03

Week three: a written plan

What I found, what I’d sequence, what each item costs and what it closes. One page, with the trade-offs stated, so the team can argue about the ranking without arguing about the facts.

04

Week four: a rhythm

Weekly critique, engineering office hours, and a decision log next to the frames. By day 30 the team knows where to find the answer without asking me.
